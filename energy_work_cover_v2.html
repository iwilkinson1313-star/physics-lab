1. Where does the energy go?

Read this first

Imagine a car moving along a flat road. The driver presses the brakes. The car slows down and stops.

We know that a moving car has energy in its kinetic energy store.

When the car slows down, this store decreases. But energy cannot simply disappear.

Write in your book · Task 1

Subtitle: Where does the energy go?

1a. Which energy store decreases as the car slows down?

1b. What do you think happens to this energy?

1c. Why do you think the brakes get warm?

Now reveal the explanation
1. The car slows down. Its kinetic energy store decreases.
2. Friction in the brakes makes the brakes warm up. The tyres and road can also warm up.
3. Energy is transferred from the car's kinetic energy store to thermal energy stores.
4. The warm brakes, tyres and road gradually transfer energy to the surroundings.

Remember: Energy is transferred, not destroyed.

2. What is work done?

Read · A new idea

In physics, we use a special phrase when a force transfers energy:

Work is done.

For example, friction is a force. As friction slows a car down, energy is transferred from the car's kinetic energy store to thermal energy stores.

We say that work is done against friction.

Work done is energy transferred. It is not another energy store.

Quick check · Choose one answer each

A. A person pushes a box across the floor. What does the force do?

B. A sliding book slows down because of friction. Where does some of its energy go?

Read · Three everyday examples
Lifting a bag: A force lifts the bag. Work is done and energy is transferred to its gravitational potential energy store.
Speeding up a trolley: A forward force moves the trolley. Work is done and its kinetic energy store increases.
Stopping a bicycle: Friction slows the bicycle. Work is done against friction and energy is transferred to thermal energy stores.

If you push a wall but it does not move, you do no work on the wall because it does not move through a distance. Your muscles still use energy.

3. Calculating work done

Read · The equation

The bigger the force, or the further an object moves in the direction of the force, the more work is done.

W = F × s

W = work done, in joules (J)

F = force, in newtons (N)

s = distance moved in the direction of the force, in metres (m)

For now, we will use forces that stay constant and act along the direction of movement.

Read · Worked example

A 20 N force pushes a box 4 m along the floor.

Equation: W = F × s
Substitute: W = 20 × 4
Answer: W = 80 J
Try it · Interactive model

Move the sliders to see how force and distance affect the work done.

20 × 4 = 80 J

4. How much force stops a car?

Read · Connect the ideas

A car has 6,000 J of kinetic energy. It brakes and stops after travelling 50 m on a flat road.

Before braking: The car has 6,000 J in its kinetic energy store.
While braking: Friction slows the car down. Work is done against the resistive forces.
After stopping: The car has 0 J of kinetic energy. The 6,000 J has been transferred, mainly to thermal energy stores.

So the work done against the resistive forces is 6,000 J.

Write in your book · Task 5

Subtitle: Stopping a car

5a. How much kinetic energy has the car lost?

5b. How much work has been done against the resistive forces?

5c. Write W = F × s and rearrange it to make F the subject.

5d. Use the distance of 50 m to calculate the average resistive force.

Hint for 5d

F = W ÷ s. You know W = 6,000 J and s = 50 m.

Worked answer · Open after trying
Decrease in kinetic energy = 6,000 J
Work done against resistive forces = 6,000 J
F = W ÷ s
F = 6,000 ÷ 50 = 120 N

The average resultant resistive force is 120 N, acting opposite to the car's movement. We assume the road is level and ignore other significant energy transfers.

Quick check

5. Practise independently

Write in your book · Task 6

Subtitle: Practice questions

For each calculation, show the equation, substitution, answer and unit.

  1. A 60 N force moves a box 7 m. Calculate the work done.
  2. A frictional force of 25 N slows a sliding object over 12 m. Calculate the energy transferred by work done against friction.
  3. A moving object has 2,400 J of kinetic energy. It stops over 8 m on a level surface. Calculate the average resistive force.
  4. A 1,000 kg car travels at 12 m/s. Calculate its kinetic energy using Eₖ = ½mv². It then stops over 36 m. Calculate the average resistive force.
  5. Explain: Why do car brakes get warm when the car slows down? Write three short sentences. Include kinetic energy store, friction, and thermal energy stores.
Hint for questions 3 and 4

When an object stops, its final kinetic energy is zero. On a level surface, the decrease in kinetic energy equals the work done against the resistive forces, under our simplifying assumptions.

Answers · Check after completing the questions
  1. W = 60 × 7 = 420 J
  2. W = 25 × 12 = 300 J
  3. F = 2,400 ÷ 8 = 300 N
  4. Eₖ = ½ × 1,000 × 12² = 72,000 J. F = 72,000 ÷ 36 = 2,000 N
  5. As the car slows down, its kinetic energy store decreases. Friction in the brakes transfers energy to thermal energy stores, making the brakes warmer. Energy is then transferred to the surroundings.
